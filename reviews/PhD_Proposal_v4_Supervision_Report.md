# Doctoral Supervision Report

## Proposal reviewed

**Translanguaging and Interactional Competence: Toward Decolonial Pedagogies in Chinese as a Second Language**  
Applicant: Jiaxin Shen  
Version reviewed: `PhD_Proposal_Revised_v4_Aotearoa_Focus.pdf` (20 pages)

## Supervisor's overall judgement

This is a serious and potentially distinctive doctoral project. Its strongest intellectual move is to refuse two easy equations: translanguaging is not automatically emancipatory, and choosing not to translanguage is not automatically evidence of oppression or deficient competence. The proposal instead treats repertoire use and non-use as situated choices whose meanings must be established empirically. That is a defensible and productive position.

The project is not yet ready in its current architecture. It presently asks one study to carry too many theoretical and empirical burdens: translanguaging ontology, NoM, LREs, interactional competence, affect, identity, learner agency, teacher agency, multimodality, raciolinguistics, assessment, institutional policy and decoloniality. The danger is not lack of sophistication; it is diffusion. A committee is likely to ask which single phenomenon is being explained and which constructs are explanatory, sensitising, descriptive or merely contextual.

**Recommendation: revise and resubmit after major conceptual narrowing.** Preserve the Aotearoa CSL focus, learner electivity, micro-interactional evidence and teacher/institutional context. Reduce the number of outcomes claimed, make decoloniality an empirically bounded analytic question, and build one explicit analytic architecture.

## What should remain at the centre

The strongest possible thesis spine is:

> This study investigates how learner electivity over multilingual and multimodal resources is interactionally negotiated in university CSL classrooms, and how teacher practices and institutional conditions shape when such choices become available, legitimate and consequential for participation.

This spine is stronger than “translanguaging develops a multidimensional interactional competence” because it names an observable phenomenon, avoids predetermining a beneficial effect, and connects micro interaction to institutional conditions without claiming to measure everything.

### Proposed one-sentence contribution

> The study contributes an empirically grounded account of **learner electivity** as an interactional and institutionally conditioned accomplishment, showing how multilingual resource use, limited use and non-use acquire different consequences for participation in Aotearoa university CSL classrooms.

Interactional competence can remain an important conversation partner, but it should not also become a second enormous construct that the thesis promises to reconceptualise across linguistic, affective, agentive, embodied and ideological dimensions.

## Priority 1: Rewrite the research questions

### Problem in the current version

The four questions on pages 2–3 operate at different scales and contain several projects:

- RQ1 asks about learner practices, reasons and stances.
- RQ2 asks how three constructs “intersect to develop” interactional competence.
- RQ3 asks about teacher interpretation, institutional pressure and effects on learner choice.
- RQ4 asks about power, culture, assessment, discourse and “the depth of any decolonial change.”

RQ2 uses developmental language (“develop”) although the design primarily observes one semester and does not specify a developmental comparison or outcome measure. RQ4 is too broad to answer rigorously and risks making decolonial change a scale that has not yet been theoretically or methodologically established.

### Recommended three-question structure

1. **How do learners and teachers interactionally make multilingual and multimodal resources available, optional, expected or sanctionable in university CSL classrooms?**

2. **How do learners take up, limit or decline these resources, and what locally demonstrable consequences do these choices have for participation, meaning negotiation and stance?**

3. **How are these interactional patterns interpreted by participants and conditioned by course goals, assessment practices and institutional language commitments?**

These questions create a coherent movement from interactional organisation, to consequences, to participant/institutional interpretation. NoM and LRE can become possible interactional phenomena found within RQ2 rather than co-equal theoretical pillars. Decoloniality becomes an interpretation tested against the findings rather than an outcome built into the question.

## Priority 2: Decide what each theory is doing

Create a one-page conceptual architecture with four levels:

| Level | Function | Recommended concepts |
|---|---|---|
| Phenomenon | What the study observes | learner electivity over multilingual/multimodal resources |
| Interactional description | How episodes unfold | turn design, repair, uptake, stance, participation; NoM/LRE where demonstrably relevant |
| Institutional explanation | What conditions availability and risk | teacher positioning, assessment, programme policy, language ideology |
| Critical interpretation | What the pattern means for justice | repertoire legitimacy, epistemic recognition, raciolinguistic positioning, bounded decolonial possibility |

Do not let every concept operate at every level. In particular:

- **NoM and LREs** should not be assumed to be pedagogical strategies; define how they will be identified in sequential data.
- **Interactional competence** requires a decision: is it an outcome, an observable collection of practices, or a field of scholarship that your findings revise? Choose one.
- **Learner electivity** is promising but presently underdefined. Distinguish it from agency, choice, preference, investment and refusal. Explain when a choice counts as meaningful under constraint.
- **Decoloniality** should not be inferred merely from multilingual resource use. Keep the five proposed dimensions, but specify what evidence would count as movement, non-movement or contradiction on each dimension.

## Priority 3: Resolve the methodology stack

### Current risk

Pages 8 and 11–12 combine classroom ethnography, CA-informed Discourse Analysis, Jeffersonian transcription, ELAN multimodal annotation, Norris/Kress-inspired Multimodal Interaction Analysis, thematic analysis, multiple-case comparison and Critical Discourse Analysis. Each is defensible individually, but together they currently look additive rather than integrated.

A CA-oriented examiner may object to predetermined coding categories such as “resistance,” “affective markers” and “ideological keywords” before participant relevance is demonstrated. A critical ethnographer may object that turn-by-turn analysis alone cannot warrant claims about decoloniality or institutional power.

### Recommended analytic architecture

1. **Ethnographic mapping:** establish classroom histories, language expectations, assessment ecology, participant repertoires and researcher positionality.
2. **Episode identification:** use broad indexing to locate moments in which resource choice becomes consequential—repair, explicit language policing, hesitation/refusal, revoicing, assessment preparation or contested participation.
3. **Multimodal sequential analysis:** analyse a purposive corpus of focal episodes turn by turn, including speech, gaze, gesture, material objects and spatial organisation.
4. **Participant interpretation:** use stimulated recall cautiously to examine participants' retrospective accounts; do not treat these accounts as transparent proof of the original moment.
5. **Institutional interpretation:** compare recurrent interactional patterns with interviews, assessment artefacts and programme documents.
6. **Critical synthesis:** only after the preceding phases, evaluate what the evidence warrants concerning repertoire legitimacy, epistemic recognition and decolonial possibility.

Replace the claim that member checking will “ensure accurate representation.” Member reflection can expose disagreement and enrich interpretation, but it cannot guarantee a single accurate account.

## Priority 4: Make the project feasible

The proposed corpus—three to four classes, 12–18 focal learners, 3–4 instructors, a semester of observation, at least 30 hours of video, repeated interviews, stimulated recall, institutional documents, ELAN annotation, multimodal microanalysis and cross-case thematic analysis—is too large if every stream receives equal analytic depth.

Recommended bounding:

- Retain 3–4 classes for ethnographic mapping.
- Designate **two focal classroom ecologies** for intensive comparison.
- Select approximately **20–30 focal interactional episodes** for full multimodal transcription rather than implying exhaustive ELAN annotation of 30 hours.
- State selection criteria before analysis while allowing theoretically important deviant cases.
- Use remaining recordings to establish distribution and context, not to make every minute part of the microanalytic corpus.
- Include negative and ambiguous cases by design.

## Priority 5: Strengthen the Aotearoa and decolonial grounding

### The major committee risk

The proposal invokes Te Tiriti-informed commitments, te reo Māori revitalisation and Indigenous epistemology, yet the empirical site is a university Chinese programme and the sample may contain few or no Māori participants. Without Māori governance, scholarship and relationships, a committee may view “decolonial” and “Indigenous epistemology” as contextual legitimacy borrowed for a project that primarily studies multilingual classroom choice.

Choose one of two defensible routes:

### Route A — Bounded critical claim

Frame the study as examining the **limits and possibilities of institutional multilingual/decolonial commitments** in CSL practice. Do not claim to enact decolonisation. Treat Te Tiriti commitments as part of the institutional discourse being studied, and describe carefully what the project cannot claim about Māori education.

### Route B — Relational and governed decolonial design

If decolonial transformation is a central contribution, develop appropriate Māori consultation, governance, research relationships and engagement with kaupapa Māori and Indigenous language revitalisation scholarship. Clarify benefit, accountability, data governance and how Māori perspectives shape the design rather than merely being sampled as learner background.

At present, Route A is more feasible and better supported by the proposed methods.

Also revise “Aotearoa is constituted as an officially multilingual society.” Legal language status, societal multilingualism and Te Tiriti obligations are different claims. State each precisely and cite the relevant official or scholarly source.

## Priority 6: Remove outcomes the design does not measure

Pages 12–14 propose possible consequences for psychological safety, motivation, target-language development, identity, emotion, participation and interactional competence. The current methods can investigate participant accounts and interactional displays related to several of these, but cannot robustly establish all as developmental outcomes.

Recommended action:

- Keep **participation, stance and locally achieved meaning** as primary interactional consequences.
- Treat affect and identity as claims requiring converging sequential and participant evidence.
- Remove “psychological safety,” “motivation” and “target-language development” as outcomes unless the study adds defensible measures and a longitudinal analytic plan.
- Replace “expected results” with **anticipated analytic contributions and rival possibilities**.

## Section-level comments

### Opening rationale, pages 1–2

**Issue:** The opening moves quickly from monolingual paradigms to cognitive engagement, emotional well-being and cultural positioning. These benefits are presented broadly before the proposal establishes its more careful non-deterministic position.

**Action:** Begin with the empirical tension in Aotearoa CSL, then introduce translanguaging as one contested way of understanding it. Move the strong benefits sentence later or qualify it.

### Literature review, pages 4–7

**Strength:** The UTT/CTT positioning is unusually explicit and gives the project a defensible ontological stance.

**Issue:** The literature review still reads as several adjacent conversations rather than an argument that produces the study. The interactional competence section arrives after the translanguaging/decolonial discussion but their conceptual relation remains asserted rather than demonstrated.

**Action:** Organise the review around three disputes: (1) what multilingual resource use is; (2) how choice is interactionally organised; (3) when that organisation has critical/decolonial significance. End each dispute with a precise unresolved inference.

### Gap and contribution, pages 7–8

**Issue:** “Few studies” and “under-theorisation” claims need a transparent basis. The proposal also claims to reconceptualise interactional competence and advance decolonial pedagogy at the same time.

**Action:** Present one primary theoretical contribution (learner electivity as interactional accomplishment), one methodological contribution (multimodal sequential evidence connected to ethnographic/institutional context), and one bounded practical contribution.

### Site and sampling, pages 8–10

**Issue:** “CSL instruction typically operates under a monolingual Mandarin immersion regime” sounds like a conclusion about the site before fieldwork and needs local evidence. Sampling learners by Māori, Pacific and Asian-diaspora backgrounds risks treating broad identities as explanatory variables.

**Action:** Recast the immersion claim as a proposition to be investigated. Sample for relevant repertoire histories, course trajectories and participation patterns; do not presume ethnic categories predict language choices.

### Data collection and analysis, pages 10–12

**Issue:** The timeline says teachers are interviewed at the start and end, while Year 2 says pre-semester and mid-semester interviews with students and instructors. Reconcile the schedule.

**Action:** Add a data-source-by-RQ matrix. For each source, state whether it provides interactional evidence, participant interpretation or institutional context.

### Ethics, page 12

**Issue:** The ethics section is too brief for intensive classroom video and teacher–student power relations.

**Action:** Address incidental participants, consent in whole-class recording, withdrawal after group interaction, identifiability in video/stills, teacher access to student decisions, researcher–teacher role, translation, culturally responsive consultation, data retention and governance.

### Contribution to supervisor, pages 14–15

**Issue:** The section is overly deferential and occasionally positions the project as simply empirically grounding the proposed supervisor's work. “Takes this further” can also sound unnecessarily competitive.

**Action:** State intellectual fit, complementary expertise and the project's independent contribution. Reduce promises about collaborative publications and grants unless requested by the application format.

### Timeline, pages 15–17

**Issue:** A four-year timeline is institutionally plausible, but the first full journal manuscript proposal during ongoing fieldwork may compete with transcription and analysis. “Full-scale” also overstates a bounded qualitative study.

**Action:** Include first-year confirmation milestones, data-management/ethics contingency, staged transcription, and a decision point for narrowing the focal corpus.

### References, pages 17–20

Conduct a full citation-reference audit. At minimum, the text cites Williams (1994) and Wang (2021), but those entries are not visible in the reference list. Check every in-text year against the final bibliography and verify forthcoming/2026 publication metadata before submission.

## Committee questions you should be able to answer

1. What is the single phenomenon this dissertation explains?
2. What does “learner electivity” add beyond agency, preference, investment or refusal?
3. How can you infer the meaning of non-use without treating interview accounts as transparent truth?
4. What evidence would show that translanguaging constrained rather than supported participation?
5. Is interactional competence an outcome, an analytic framework or the object being reconceptualised?
6. Why is NoM/LRE terminology necessary if your primary analysis is multimodal and critical?
7. What makes the project decolonial rather than inclusive multilingual pedagogy?
8. What responsibilities follow from invoking te reo Māori and Te Tiriti when the project is located in CSL?
9. How will you prevent critical interpretation from overriding participants' own orientations?
10. What part of the project would you remove first if access or transcription capacity were reduced by half?

## Six-step revision sequence

1. Write the one-sentence phenomenon and contribution; do not edit prose elsewhere yet.
2. Replace the four RQs with a coherent three-question sequence.
3. Build the four-level conceptual architecture and assign every construct one function.
4. Decide between the bounded critical route and the relational decolonial route.
5. Rewrite methodology around focal episodes and the five-stage analytic logic.
6. Only then revise the introduction, gap, expected contribution, timeline and references for consistency.

## First supervision task

Before revising the full proposal, write no more than 250 words answering:

> What exactly is learner electivity, how can it be observed in classroom interaction, and what would distinguish meaningful electivity from a choice produced by unequal constraints?

This answer should define the phenomenon, state observable indicators, identify one rival interpretation and explain what evidence could disconfirm your account.
